## The centrepiece — the defect this round exists to fix

The author found this in their own work, mid-round, and it is the best finding of the round:

> Leave `SINK_EVASIONS` **completely intact** — all 27 entries, size pin satisfied — and change only
> its consumption loop to `for (… of SINK_EVASIONS.slice(0, 0))`. **GREEN**, 82 checks / 131
> assertions, `tsc --noEmit` 0.

The size pin is computed from the **declared array**; the loop iterates a **different expression**.
Nothing connected them. **Twenty-nine loops in the file had that shape.** `[REPORTED — verify the 29]`

Their fix: `consumeFixtureTable(name, table, expected)`, a **generator** that checks the count at the
point of consumption and records the loop as having run only after yielding every entry, plus
`EXPECTED_FIXTURE_LOOPS` and a census in `run()`. They applied it to **six** of the twenty-nine.

### What I want you to establish about it, in order of value

1. **Is the new machinery itself neuterable?** This is the whole game. A pin that catches
   `.slice(0, 0)` but is itself defeatable by an equally cheap edit has moved the defect, not closed
   it. Attack `consumeFixtureTable`, `EXPECTED_FIXTURE_LOOPS` and the `run()` census directly. Their
   own T-3b (replace the helper body with `yield* table`) went RED — good — but that is one mutation
   chosen by the author. Find the cheapest edit that leaves the suite GREEN with a loop not actually
   consuming its table.

2. **Verify the docblock refutation independently, because it is the most interesting technical
   claim in the report and it is load-bearing for the design.** They claim an early `break` invokes
   the generator's `return()`, so execution never resumes past the `yield`, so the trailing
   `yielded !== expected` arm is **unreachable from a break** — and therefore it is the **census**,
   not the trailing arm, that catches an abandoned loop. If that is right, the trailing arm is doing
   less than it appears to and the census is a single point of failure. **So: what pins the census?**
   The report concedes "nothing pins the two `run()` censuses themselves, or the two totals." Is that
   an acceptable terminal position or is it the same defect one level up? Say which, and why.

3. **The T-3 equivalent mutant.** They disarmed only the length arm, left the trailing arm live, got
   GREEN, and recorded it as an equivalent mutant rather than a finding — reasoning that the
   surviving arm still detects every wrong size. **Given claim 2 above, is that reasoning consistent
   with itself?** If the trailing arm is unreachable from a `break`, what exactly is the surviving arm
   covering, and is "detection is preserved" true for all inputs or only for the ones they tried?
   This is precisely the arm-masking shape that has bitten this project repeatedly (rule 26).

4. **The 23 unwrapped loops.** The author states plainly that 23 of 29 remain neuterable by the
   `.slice(0, 0)` mutation, and gives a scope rationale I find reasonable. **Verify the residual is
   what they say it is** — not larger, not smaller, and in particular confirm the denominator 29 by
   your own enumeration with your own method. Then answer the question they did not: **are the 23
   they left the low-value ones?** Six evasion tables were chosen because "a skipped loop means a rule
   is no longer known to catch anything." Sample the remaining 23 and report whether any of them
   guards something equally load-bearing. A defensible stopping point and an arbitrary one look

5. **The six wraps, individually.** For each of the six, neuter its loop and confirm RED **with a
